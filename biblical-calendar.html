Biblical calendar
Moadim of YHWH

Why do we use the observed calendar?

In Kodesh, the feast dates follow the signs Scripture gives: the new moon as it is seen and the aviv barley in the land of Israel. We don't use the calculated calendar that was fixed in the days of Hillel II.

1 · What the Torah says

Elohim placed the lights of the heavens to mark the appointed times. The word we translate “seasons” is מוֹעֲדִים (moadim), the same word used for the feasts of Leviticus 23.

“Let there be lights in the expanse of the sky to divide the day from the night; and let them be for signs to mark seasons, days, and years.”Genesis 1:14

The Hebrew month, חֹדֶשׁ (chodesh), comes from the root “new” (חָדָשׁ): the month begins when the moon is renewed. That is why the Bible speaks of “the new moon” as a day everyone knew (1 Samuel 20:5; 2 Kings 4:23; Ezekiel 46:1).

And the first month of the year is not a fixed date: it is the month when the barley is אָבִיב (aviv), that is, in the ear and about to ripen.

“Today you go out in the month Aviv.”Exodus 13:4 · see also Exodus 12:2 and Deuteronomy 16:1
“The flax and the barley were struck, for the barley had ripened” (in Hebrew, אָבִיב).Exodus 9:31

2 · How it was done in Temple times

While the Temple and the Sanhedrin stood in Jerusalem, the month was declared when witnesses arrived who had seen the crescent moon (as the Mishnah describes in tractate Rosh Hashanah, chapters 1 and 2). And when the barley and the season were not ready, a month was added to the year before Aviv.

3 · What changed with Hillel II

After the destruction of the Temple, with the people scattered and under persecution, it was no longer possible to notify every community in time. According to tradition, around AD 359 the patriarch Hillel II made public a calculated calendar, which is the one most of the Jewish people use today. It was a practical solution to keep a scattered people united, and we respect it.

Hillel II calendar

  • The month begins with the average conjunction (the molad), which cannot be seen.
  • Seven fixed leap years in every 19-year cycle, without looking at the barley.
  • Postponement rules (dechiyot): Yom Teruah never falls on a Sunday, Wednesday or Friday, even if that moves the date by one or two days.
  • Firstfruits on Nisan 16 and Shavuot on Sivan 6.

Observed calendar (Kodesh)

  • The month begins on the evening the first crescent is seen from Jerusalem.
  • The year begins with the month in which the barley is aviv in Israel.
  • No postponements: the feast falls on the day the moon indicates.
  • Firstfruits on the Sunday during Unleavened Bread, and Shavuot 50 days later, always on a Sunday.

4 · Firstfruits and Shavuot: “the day after the Sabbath”

The Torah gives no date for Firstfruits or Shavuot: it commands that they be counted from a day of the week.

“You shall count from the next day after the Sabbath, from the day that you brought the sheaf of the wave offering; seven full weeks shall be completed. Until the next day after the seventh Sabbath you shall count fifty days.”Leviticus 23:15–16

Counting “seven full weeks” up to “the next day after the seventh Sabbath” always lands on a Sunday. In the 1st century the Pharisees understood it as Nisan 16; the Sadducees, and later the Karaites, as the Sunday. It was on a Sunday, “the first day of the week,” that Yeshua rose as the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep (1 Corinthians 15:20).

5 · How the app calculates the dates

For each month, Kodesh calculates the first evening on which the crescent can be seen with the naked eye from Jerusalem (Odeh's astronomical visibility criterion). The month of Aviv is estimated each year and confirmed with the barley reports from Israel. If the moon is seen a day earlier or later, or if the barley is not yet aviv, the dates are corrected and reach the app without needing an app update.

Remember that the biblical day begins at sunset: each feast begins the evening before the date shown.

Many brothers, sisters and congregations keep the feasts by the Hillel II calendar out of unity with the Jewish people, and that is honorable too. Our goal is not to divide, but to return to the signs of Scripture. “Let no one therefore judge you in eating, or in drinking, or with respect to a feast day or a new moon or a Sabbath day” (Colossians 2:16).
